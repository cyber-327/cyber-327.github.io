CYBER 327: Digital Communications

Course Description

Every digital message has to travel through the physical world. In CYBER 327, we will build, measure, and investigate the signals that make digital communication possible.

Along the way, we will see how noise, interference, hardware changes, and attackers can affect a communication link. Hands-on labs connect the theory to real measurements, useful tools, and cybersecurity scenarios in a safe, controlled environment.

Prerequisites

  • Calculus
  • First course in physics
  • Basic circuit and programming familiarity are helpful but not assumed beyond course prerequisites

Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, you will get to know these topics:

  • Electrical and circuit foundations for communication hardware
  • Signals in the time and frequency domains
  • Noise, bandwidth, SNR, filtering, and channel capacity
  • Analog and digital modulation, sampling, coding, BER, and receiver behavior
  • Wired, wireless, optical, serial, cellular, and satellite communication systems
  • Signal measurement, SDR, interference, spoofing, tamper evidence, and other physical-layer cybersecurity concerns
  • Safe, legal, and evidence-based investigation of communication systems

Labs

Labs are central to the course. They connect digital communications principles to real measurements, signal behavior, and lawful cybersecurity-relevant analysis.

  • You may work alone or in a group of up to 3.
  • Take photos of your setups as proof in your lab reports.
  • Report any equipment problems.
  • Be safe!

Generally, all labs are due the day before the next lab will happen (Thursday at 11:59)

Homework

Homework supports fluency with calculations, concepts, and interpretation. Students should show work and reasoning, not only final answers. Homework may include textbook problems, short analysis prompts, signal interpretation exercises, or preparation for labs.

Retransmission And Error Correction

Everyone begins the semester with four retransmission credits. Each credit extends an eligible homework assignment or individual lab report by 24 hours. You may use no more than two credits on one assignment, and you do not need to provide a reason or request advance permission. Indicate the credits used when you submit the work.

After your retransmission credits are exhausted, eligible late work loses 10% per calendar day, up to a maximum deduction of 50%. Work cannot be submitted after solutions are released, after an assignment-specific closing date, or after the final course deadline.

You also have two error-correction opportunities during the semester. You may revise an eligible homework assignment or lab report within seven calendar days after receiving feedback and recover up to half of the points lost for correctable technical errors. Error correction does not replace missing work and does not apply to quizzes, exams, in-lab participation, the Excellence Project, or final course deadlines.

Approved accommodations and instructor-approved exceptional circumstances are handled separately from these credits.

Quizzes And Tests

The course provides 12 short quiz opportunities; the best 10 scores count equally toward the 10% quiz category. This built-in erasure tolerance accommodates two missed or unusually poor quiz results without requiring routine make-ups. A missed quiz receives a zero and is automatically handled by the two dropped scores when available. Additional make-ups require an accommodation or instructor-approved circumstance.

Quizzes should normally take 5-8 minutes and occur approximately once per week, preferably on Monday or Wednesday rather than immediately before the Friday lab. Quizzes may be open-note or open-resource when the questions emphasize application, calculation, interpretation, and reasoning rather than lookup. The published schedule will identify quiz dates and coverage.

Midterm tests may be open-resource when questions require students to apply communications principles and defend conclusions. Dates, time limits, and permitted resources will be announced in class and on the course site.

Excellence Project

The applied project is worth 5% of the course grade and will use a published rubric. Possible directions include:

  • Build or improve a small communication experiment.
  • Analyze a signal or link using lawful, receive-only or lab-owned data.
  • Extend a lab with additional measurements or documentation.
  • Compare media or modulation choices for a realistic secure-link scenario.
  • Create a student-facing study guide or visualization for a difficult communication concept.

Project scope, team size, due date, and the rubric will be published before the proposal is due.

Professionalism, Participation, And Attendance

Professionalism includes attendance, preparation, participation, technical care, ethical behavior, teamwork, and respect for lab equipment and classmates. Students are expected to attend class, arrive on time, participate constructively, and complete work even when it is difficult.

Participation matters because communication and security work require clear reasoning, careful questioning, and evidence-based discussion. Questions are encouraged.

Unexcused absences may affect the professionalism grade; excused absences should be communicated to the instructor.

Final Exam

The final examination is worth 15% of the course grade. Its date and time will be announced.

The final assessment will emphasize synthesis: communication-link limitations, media selection, modulation and coding tradeoffs, measurement evidence, cybersecurity applications, and safe/legal boundaries.
