Course Description

CYBER 327 develops the physical and signal-level foundations of digital communication systems. The course begins with electrical foundations, measurement integrity, circuit behavior, impedance, resonance, and filtering. It then connects those foundations to spectrum analysis, noise, SNR, Shannon capacity, modulation, receiver behavior, ADC/DAC conversion, error detection and coding, QAM, BER, SDR, wireless systems, serial interfaces, network media, fiber optics, antennas, and link budgets.

Cybersecurity applications explain why communication fundamentals matter: how signals can be observed, classified, disrupted, misread, spoofed, protected, or used as evidence. Labs emphasize lawful, controlled, lab-owned, receive-only, or instructor-provided signal activities while keeping communication theory and measurement at the center.

Prerequisites

  • Calculus
  • First course in physics
  • Basic circuit and programming familiarity are helpful but not assumed beyond course prerequisites

Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

  • Describe the major blocks of a communication system and the limitations imposed by real media, noise, bandwidth, and hardware.
  • Analyze basic DC and AC circuits used to model communication behavior, including resistance, reactance, impedance, resonance, and filtering.
  • Explain how SNR, noise, bandwidth, and Shannon's Law limit communication capacity and reliability.
  • Compare amplitude, frequency, phase, and digital modulation methods using bandwidth, noise immunity, complexity, and resilience tradeoffs.
  • Interpret time-domain and frequency-domain signal evidence, including harmonics, sidebands, spectral occupancy, and waterfall displays.
  • Explain how ADC/DAC sampling, quantization, aliasing, and reconstruction affect digital representation of analog signals.
  • Distinguish error detection, correction, authentication, and encryption at a high level, and explain why CRC/checksum mechanisms do not prove trust.
  • Interpret constellation plots, eye diagrams, BER, and link metrics as evidence of channel quality and impairment.
  • Compare wired, wireless, optical, serial, and network media using capacity, attenuation, interference, access, tamper risk, and security implications.
  • Apply evidence-based reasoning to lawful signal observation: classify signals, document evidence, state confidence, and recognize ethical/legal boundaries.
  • Identify physical-layer and signal-level risks such as interference, jamming, interception, spoofing, measurement error, coupling, cable faults, and fiber tamper evidence.
  • Design or evaluate a communication link by selecting media, modulation, coding, measurement strategy, and security controls appropriate to a scenario.

Specialized Equipment And Tool Policy

Some labs may use signal-analysis or cybersecurity-adjacent tools such as RTL-SDRs, USB logic analyzers, USB-UART adapters, isolated Ethernet taps, Wi-Fi analysis tools, or instructor-controlled demonstrations with tools such as HackRF, Flipper Zero, or WiFi Pineapple.

All tool use must follow these restrictions:

  • Use only lab-owned devices, isolated lab networks, instructor-provided captures, or receive-only public signals.
  • Do not target campus production networks, third-party devices, student personal devices, access-control systems, cellular infrastructure, or public infrastructure.
  • Do not collect credentials, clone real badges or remotes, replay third-party signals, or interfere with production RF systems.
  • Transmit-capable tools require explicit instructor control and must use approved shielding, cabled setups, dummy loads, or other safe containment.
  • Treat tools as ways to understand communication principles, not as shortcuts to exploitation.

Professionalism, Participation, And Attendance

Professionalism includes attendance, preparation, participation, technical care, ethical behavior, teamwork, and respect for lab equipment and classmates. Students are expected to attend class, arrive on time, participate constructively, and complete work even when it is difficult.

Participation matters because communication and security work require clear reasoning, careful questioning, and evidence-based discussion. Questions are encouraged.

Unexcused absences may affect the professionalism grade; excused absences should be communicated to the instructor.

Homework

Homework supports fluency with calculations, concepts, and interpretation. Students should show work and reasoning, not only final answers. Homework may include textbook problems, short analysis prompts, signal interpretation exercises, or preparation for labs.

Unless otherwise announced, late homework may lose 10% per school day up to a maximum deduction of 50%.

Quizzes And Tests

Quizzes and tests should evaluate communication fundamentals and cybersecurity applications without relying on private prior assessment content.

The course will offer 12 short quiz opportunities. The lowest two quiz scores will be dropped, leaving 10 quizzes that count equally toward the 10% quiz category. Quizzes should normally take 5-8 minutes and occur approximately once per week, preferably on Monday or Wednesday rather than immediately before the Friday lab. The published schedule will identify quiz dates and coverage.

Quizzes may be open-note or open-resource when the questions emphasize application, calculation, interpretation, and reasoning rather than lookup. Unless an accommodation or approved exception applies, a missed quiz may serve as one of the two dropped scores.

Possible quiz/test emphases:

  • Circuit and measurement foundations.
  • SNR, dB, Shannon capacity, and spectrum interpretation.
  • Modulation, ADC/DAC, aliasing, CRC/FEC, QAM, BER, and eye diagrams.
  • SDR/wireless awareness, serial interfaces, transmission lines, fiber, antennas, and link budgets.
  • Ethical and legal boundaries for signal observation and tool use.

Midterm tests may be open-resource when questions require students to apply communications principles and defend conclusions. Dates, time limits, and permitted resources will be announced in class and on the course site.

Lab Policies

Labs are central to the course. They connect digital communications principles to real measurements, signal behavior, and lawful cybersecurity-relevant analysis.

Lab expectations:

  • Work in approved groups according to instructor/TA policy.
  • Use only approved lab equipment and lab-owned or instructor-provided devices.
  • Record enough evidence for another technical reader to understand what was measured and why.
  • Report equipment problems promptly.
  • Follow all safety, electrical, RF, optical, and legal/ethical guardrails.
  • Do not perform unauthorized monitoring, transmission, replay, credential collection, or interaction with third-party systems.

Unless otherwise announced, late labs may lose 10% per school day up to a maximum deduction of 50%.

Lab Reports

Unless a specific lab handout says otherwise, lab reports should include:

  • Lab number and title.
  • Objective.
  • Equipment used, including model numbers when available.
  • Procedure summary.
  • Measurements, screenshots, tables, diagrams, or calculations as appropriate.
  • Comparison of expected and measured results.
  • Interpretation of findings.
  • Cybersecurity relevance when applicable.
  • Safety/legal guardrails followed.
  • Conclusion.
  • Names and date.

Applied Communications Project

The proposed applied project is worth 5% of the course grade and should use a published rubric. Possible project directions include:

  • Build or improve a small communication experiment.
  • Analyze a signal or link using lawful, receive-only or lab-owned data.
  • Extend a lab with additional measurements or documentation.
  • Compare media or modulation choices for a realistic secure-link scenario.
  • Create a student-facing study guide or visualization for a difficult communication concept.

Project scope, team size, due date, and the rubric will be published before the proposal is due.
