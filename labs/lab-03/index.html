Transformer Coupling and Breadboard Crosstalk

Compare useful transformer coupling with unintended crosstalk between nearby wires, then test two ways to reduce it

Learning Outcomes

By the End of This Lab

  • Identify the two windings of a transformer and verify that there is no direct DC path between them
  • Measure primary and secondary AC voltage and compare their ratio with the transformer expectation
  • Compare intentional transformer coupling with unintended crosstalk between nearby conductors
  • Measure how conductor orientation and receiver resistance affect observed crosstalk
  • Use measured evidence to identify supported path-side and receiver-side improvements without overstating the result

The Big Picture

What You Are Doing

  1. Begin with intentional magnetic coupling: identify a transformer primary and secondary, verify the isolation boundary with power disconnected, and measure its AC voltage ratio.
  2. Build a source line that carries a known signal and a nearby neighbor line that represents a quiet, legitimate circuit affected by crosstalk.
  3. Verify that the neighbor-line waveform follows the source and disappears when the source is disabled.
  4. Compare parallel conductors with a 90-degree crossing while keeping instrument settings fixed.
  5. Replace only the neighbor-line resistance and measure whether lower receiver impedance reduces the observed crosstalk.
  6. Use the measurements to explain the difference between useful coupling, unintended crosstalk, and tested path-side and receiver-side improvements.

Before Lab

Preparation

  • Review transformer windings, turns ratio, galvanic isolation, coupling, crosstalk, peak-to-peak voltage, RMS voltage, and amplitude ratio
  • Bring a calculator and open the Lab 3 report before beginning
  • Recall that resistance and continuity are measured only with the source disabled and the component disconnected from power
  • Keep the DMM leads in `COM` and `V/Ω`; this lab does not use either current jack or AC-current mode
  • Recall that oscilloscope ground clips connect only to generator ground or the instructor-designated circuit common
  • Do not substitute personal electronics, wireless devices, building wiring, or unknown powered equipment for the approved source

Bench Setup

Equipment and Materials

  • Instructor-approved, labeled low-voltage transformer and station card
  • Digital multimeter with working resistance and AC-voltage modes
  • Two-channel oscilloscope with two 10x probes
  • Function generator with BNC-to-clip lead or approved output adapter
  • Breadboard and ordinary jumper leads
  • Two similar insulated jumper or hookup wires, approximately `15–30 cm` long
  • Instructor-approved transformer series resistor, source-line load resistor, and two neighbor-line resistors
  • Ruler and calculator

Follow These Steps

Lab Walkthrough

Prepare the Station

  1. Open the Lab 3 report and enter your name and lab partners.
  2. Identify the transformer, station card, approved resistors, breadboard, and two long insulated conductors. Do not substitute resistor values or transformer windings.
  3. Put the DMM black lead in COM and the red lead in V/Ω. Confirm that neither lead is in a current jack. This lab uses resistance and AC-voltage modes only.
  4. Keep the function-generator output disabled until a step explicitly tells you to enable it. Use only the waveform, frequency, amplitude, and resistor printed on the station card.

Part 1: Observe Intentional Coupling in a Transformer

Evidence Photo

You will take one evidence photograph in this part. Before disconnecting the powered transformer setup, capture the transformer, DMM AC-voltage reading, and station card in the same frame.

  1. Disconnect the transformer from the function generator. Set the DMM to resistance and identify the two terminals that form the primary winding and the two that form the secondary winding. Record both winding resistances in Table 1.
  2. With the transformer still unpowered, measure between one primary terminal and one secondary terminal. Record OL or the meter's out-of-range indication. If the meter reports a low resistance between windings, stop and notify the instructor.
  3. Return the DMM to AC-voltage mode. Connect the function generator, approved series resistor, and transformer primary exactly as shown on the station card. Leave the secondary connected only to the DMM when its voltage is being measured.
  4. Program the instructor-posted sine-wave settings while the generator output remains disabled. Ask for a connection check before first power-up.
  5. Enable the output. Measure AC voltage directly across the primary winding and record Vp. Move only the DMM measurement leads to the secondary winding and record Vs; do not move the generator or resistor connections.
  6. Calculate the measured voltage ratio Vs / Vp and compare it with the expected ratio printed on the station card. Small differences are expected in a real transformer.
  7. Take the required photograph, disable the generator output, and disconnect the transformer before continuing.

Part 2: Build the Source and Neighbor Lines

  1. Disable the generator output and change it to the instructor-posted crosstalk waveform, frequency, amplitude, offset, and display mode.
  2. Build the source-line path shown below: generator output, long source conductor, approved load resistor, and circuit common. The load gives the source signal a defined return path.
  3. Build the neighbor-line path with the second long conductor. It represents a quiet, legitimate signal line, not an unauthorized wire. Connect one end through the posted high-value neighbor-line resistor to circuit common and connect Channel 2 to the other end. The neighbor line must have no direct signal-wire connection to the generator output.
  4. Anchor the two long conductors parallel to each other across the breadboard. Keep the posted baseline spacing and prevent bare conductors from touching.
  5. Set both physical probes and both oscilloscope channels to 10x and DC coupling. Connect both probe ground clips only to circuit common. Connect Channel 1 to the source line and Channel 2 to the neighbor line.
  6. Ask for a connection check before enabling the generator.

Part 3: Establish and Verify the Crosstalk Baseline

Evidence Photo

You will take one baseline evidence photograph in this part. Before moving a wire, capture the parallel conductors and both oscilloscope waveforms with channel scales and measurements visible.

  1. Enable the generator. Trigger from Channel 1 and verify the source-line Vpp and frequency. Adjust Channel 2 only enough to obtain a stable neighbor-line waveform, then record neighbor-line Vpp.
  2. Confirm that the neighbor-line waveform repeats with the source-line frequency or transitions. Calculate the baseline ratio Vneighbor / Vsource.
  3. Disable the generator output without changing the oscilloscope. Verify that the neighbor-line waveform falls to the posted noise-floor range, then re-enable the source and confirm that the baseline returns.
  4. Take the required baseline photograph before moving either conductor.

Part 4: Change the Coupling Path

  1. Keep the waveform, resistors, probe factors, scope scales, bandwidth, and acquisition settings fixed. Disable the generator before moving conductors.
  2. Reposition the neighbor conductor so it crosses the source conductor at approximately 90 degrees instead of running parallel. Keep the crossing point and wire lengths on the breadboard as shown below.
  3. Re-enable the generator and record source-line and neighbor-line Vpp. Calculate the new Vneighbor / Vsource ratio.
  4. Return the neighbor line to the parallel baseline and verify that the original reading returns within the posted tolerance.
  5. State whether the 90-degree crossing reduced observed crosstalk under the tested conditions.

Part 5: Change the Neighbor-Line Resistance

Evidence Photo

You will take one mitigation evidence photograph in this part. Capture the condition with the lowest repeatable neighbor-line voltage, including the physical circuit and readable Channel 2 measurement.

  1. Restore the parallel-wire baseline. Confirm that the high-value neighbor-line resistor is installed and record the neighbor-line Vpp.
  2. Disable the generator. Replace only the neighbor-line resistor with the posted lower-value resistor; do not move either long conductor or change an instrument setting.
  3. Re-enable the generator and record source-line and neighbor-line Vpp. Calculate neighbor-line voltage reduction using 100(1 - Vlow / Vhigh)%.
  4. Explain whether the result supports lower neighbor-line impedance as a receiver-side mitigation in this circuit. Do not claim that the same resistor is appropriate for every communication system because it may also load an intended signal.
  5. Take the required mitigation photograph before dismantling the circuit.

Finish and Submit

  1. Complete all three content tables and verify units, transformer ratio, source settings, crosstalk ratios, and neighbor-line voltage reduction.
  2. Complete the Evidence section with the transformer measurement, parallel-wire baseline, and lowest-crosstalk condition.
  3. Complete the Conclusion in your own words. Explain intentional versus unintended coupling, compare the path-side and receiver-side changes, and state one limit on what the evidence proves.
  4. Disable the generator output. Return probes and channels to 10x, remove student-installed leads and components, and restore the transformer, breadboard, jumpers, and resistors to the station inventory.
  5. Save the completed report, export it as a PDF, and submit the PDF through Learning Suite.

Why This Lab Matters

  • A transformer demonstrates that useful information and energy can cross a boundary without a direct conductive path
  • A conductor carrying an intended signal can produce a measurable pattern on a nearby conductor without a direct signal-wire connection
  • Conductor orientation and receiver impedance affect how much crosstalk is observed
  • Spacing, routing, shielding, and receiver design can reduce interference, but a result from one breadboard setup does not prove containment in every environment
  • Trustworthy emissions evidence requires fixed settings, a verified source, repeatable baselines, and explicit limits on the conclusion
