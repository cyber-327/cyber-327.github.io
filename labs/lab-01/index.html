Lab 01 · Week 1 · September 4, 2026

Hardware Tamper Hunt and Series-Circuit Evidence

Ohm's Law, resistor tolerance, series current, voltage dividers, power, and prediction-versus-measurement evidence

Scheduled duration: 2 hours after Friday lecture

Learning Outcomes

By the End of This Lab

  • Measure resistance, voltage, and current with a DMM using the correct connection method
  • Predict and verify current and voltage drops in a low-voltage series circuit
  • Evaluate whether measured components and circuit results are consistent with tolerance and instrument uncertainty
  • Locate an altered component, hidden resistance, reference-path fault, or source mismatch from node-voltage evidence
  • Write a claim-evidence-reasoning conclusion that distinguishes direct observations from supported inference

The Big Picture

What You Are Doing

  1. Build a known three-resistor series circuit and verify that it behaves as predicted
  2. Use the measured values from that circuit as a trusted baseline or electrical fingerprint
  3. Visit two instructor-prepared fault stations built from the same intended circuit, but with one concealed change
  4. Compare voltages point by point, locate the first segment that differs from the baseline, and support a diagnosis with evidence

Before Lab

Preparation

  • Review V = I R, P = V I, series resistance, and the voltage-divider relationship
  • Review the difference between measuring voltage in parallel and current in series
  • Bring a calculator and open the individual report template before lab
  • Complete the baseline predictions for a 5.00 V source and 330 ohm, 1.0 kilohm, and 3.3 kilohm series resistors
  • Read all safety limits before energizing a circuit

Bench Setup

Assigned Equipment and Data

  • DMM with intact voltage, resistance, and current functions
  • Current-limited DC power supply
  • Solderless breadboard and jumper leads
  • 330 ohm, 1.0 kilohm, and 3.3 kilohm resistors, 5 percent or better
  • Instructor-prepared Lab 1 fault stations or fault adapters

Optional or Specialized

  • second DMM for simultaneous source and node measurements

Circuit and Limits

Use This Configuration

  • Baseline path: supply positive to circuit input to R1 330 ohm to node A to R2 1.0 kilohm to node B to R3 3.3 kilohm to node G to supply ground
  • Set the source to 5.00 V nominal with a 25 mA current limit; never exceed 5.20 V or 25 mA
  • Measure node voltages relative to supply ground unless a step explicitly requests a component voltage drop
  • De-energize the circuit before resistance measurements or rewiring
  • Use the DMM current input only when the meter is intentionally inserted in series; return the lead to the voltage input immediately afterward

Measurement Map

What Each Voltage Tells You

Supply voltage
Confirms the voltage available at the power-supply terminals
Circuit-input voltage
At a prepared fault station, confirms the voltage that reaches R1 and reveals a possible drop before the resistor chain
Node A voltage
Shows the voltage remaining after R1
Node B voltage
Shows the voltage remaining after R2
Node G voltage
Should be near 0 V and reveals a possible problem in the return path
Resistor voltage drops
Calculate R1 from input minus A, R2 from A minus B, and R3 from B minus G

Follow These Steps

Lab Walkthrough

  1. Set up safely: Record your team and equipment, inspect the DMM leads, and set the disabled supply to 5.00 V with a 25 mA current limit
  2. Measure the parts: With power disconnected, measure R1, R2, and R3 and decide whether each value is within its marked tolerance
  3. Predict the baseline: Using the measured resistor values, calculate total resistance, series current, resistor voltage drops, node voltages, and maximum resistor power
  4. Build and check the known circuit: Assemble R1, R2, and R3 in series using the published labels; ask a teammate to trace the complete path before enabling power
  5. Record its electrical fingerprint: Measure source voltage, series current, and nodes A, B, and G; calculate each resistor drop and compare the results with your predictions
  6. Investigate fault station 1: Measure source voltage, circuit-input voltage, and nodes A, B, and G without opening or resistance-testing the station; find the first meaningful departure from the baseline and record a claim, evidence, reasoning, confidence, and an alternative explanation
  7. Investigate fault station 2: Repeat the same point-by-point comparison and support a second diagnosis from the measured pattern
  8. Finish safely: Disable and zero the source, restore the DMM leads to the voltage input, reset the bench as directed, and complete your individual report

Submission

Required Evidence and Deliverables

  • Completed individual Word report identifying your team and role
  • Baseline section containing component measurements, predictions, circuit diagram, measured voltages and current, percent differences, and one power calculation
  • Two fault-station sections containing raw voltages, baseline comparisons, and a claim-evidence-reasoning diagnosis with confidence and an alternative explanation
  • Answers to the analysis questions and a final statement explaining what the evidence supports, what remains uncertain, and what additional measurement would help

Completion standard: another technical reader should be able to understand what was tested, reproduce the important settings, and see why the evidence supports the conclusion.

Analysis

Questions for the Individual Report

  • Why must resistance be measured with the circuit de-energized?
  • Why can placing an ammeter directly across the supply damage the meter or open its fuse?
  • Which node-voltage pattern would suggest an altered resistor rather than a lower source voltage?
  • How can a voltage drop across a wire or adapter reveal hidden series resistance?
  • Why can a changed ground or return path make every downstream voltage appear wrong?
  • When is a percent difference evidence of a fault, and when could it be consistent with tolerance or measurement uncertainty?
  • How could a small analog voltage change cause a digital receiver to interpret a different logic state near its threshold?

Why It Matters

  • Hardware tamper changes measurable electrical behavior before any software layer is involved
  • Node-by-node comparison is a basic communications troubleshooting method: establish a baseline, locate the first meaningful deviation, and state confidence
  • A trustworthy diagnosis separates observed values from an inferred cause and records alternative explanations

Safety and Scope

  • Use only lab-owned circuits and instructor-prepared fault stations
  • Keep all work at or below 5.20 V and 25 mA with current limiting enabled
  • Disable power before resistance measurements, rewiring, or moving between stations
  • Never place a DMM in current mode directly across a voltage source
  • Do not open, bypass, or dismantle hidden-fault adapters
  • Report damaged leads, unexpected heating, odor, or unstable supply behavior immediately
